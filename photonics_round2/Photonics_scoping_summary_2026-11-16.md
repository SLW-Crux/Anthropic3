# Photonics in Asia Pacific outside China: preliminary scoping

**Scoping study. Status at 16 November 2026. For consideration, not for decision.**

## What this is

A structured desk study of whether and where a private investor could invest in photonics in Asia Pacific outside China. It covered the full value chain, from materials to test equipment, and tested the investor's view that demand will outrun supply for long enough to matter, and that buyers will increasingly turn away from Chinese suppliers.

The work behind it:

- More than 1,000 sources logged, including company filings from Taiwan, Japan, Korea and the US, regulator documents and earnings materials.
- About 150 companies listed across the region, about 75 of them screened against the ownership and manufacturing rule.
- Four financial models, each rebuilt independently and found to reproduce.
- A red-team review and a separate audit of the whole record.

It is a scoping study. It narrows the field and identifies what decides the outcome. It is not a conclusive test of the thesis, because it relied on public sources and did not reach most private companies (see "What was not examined").

## The investor's premise

The mandate rests on one assumption about the world: **buyers and governments outside China will increasingly prefer, or be required, to buy photonics from suppliers with no Chinese ownership or manufacturing.** Preference may come first, through customers' own supply-chain policies, and restriction later, through regulation.

If that holds, suppliers free of Chinese ties gain a lasting advantage, and capacity of that kind becomes scarce. The rule excluding Chinese shareholders and manufacturing is therefore the core of the strategy and not a constraint added to it: it selects the companies the premise favours.

The study treated this as the investor's assumption and tested how far today's evidence supports it (see "The premise against the evidence").

## The question and the mandate

- Where does demand for photonics outrun supply for long enough to matter, which suppliers are free of Chinese ties, and which of them can be reached?
- Asia Pacific only, excluding mainland China, Hong Kong and Macau. No company with Chinese shareholders, manufacturing in China, or manufacturing with Chinese companies.
- Minority stakes with influence, with a cheque of up to US$500m. No unproven markets or products.
- Return target, holding period and fund location were not set. Results were measured against a reference of 2.0x and 15% a year.

## What the research supports

- **Demand is large and growing.** Optics for AI data centres is a market of roughly US$18–23bn in 2026. Two internal models put 2030 at US$42–56bn, mostly on assumed inputs.
- **Supply is short in specific steps:** indium-phosphide substrates, laser wafers and the best laser chips. Buyers have signed supply contracts of four to six years, the longest to 2031, and are prepaying for capacity.
- **Profit sits with the scarce steps.** Laser makers earn margins above 40% at present; module assemblers about 10%.
- **Listed companies are priced for this.** Minority stakes at November 2026 prices do not reach 2.0x in any central case.
- **Few regional suppliers are free of Chinese ties today.** Most of those screened have a plant, a partner or a shareholder in China. That makes clean capacity rare, which is the point of the premise, and it also leaves few companies to invest in. No company yet has a verified pass.

## What is not established

- **How long the shortage lasts.** "Through 2028" is a judgement at low to medium confidence. Nothing in the record supports or rules out a shortage of 15–20 years.
- **True demand beyond 2030.** No sourced forecast was built.
- **Wafer and laser prices after 2027.** No public source gives a price path. This is the assumption the one lead depends on.

## The premise against the evidence

**In its favour**

- A US rule adopted in July 2026 already restricts equipment containing components from listed Chinese producers.
- Adding optical modules to such rules is under active debate; large buyers are lobbying against it, which shows the proposal is taken seriously.
- Customers are paying to secure non-Chinese supply: one chip maker has put about US$2bn into each of two US laser makers, and Western module makers are building capacity in Malaysia.
- Chinese module makers are setting up production outside China, which suggests their customers already ask for it.

**Against it, or not yet shown**

- No rule in force covers optical modules, and the large Chinese module makers are reportedly not on the restricted list.
- The follow-on rule now proposed tests where a product is made and favours US plants. As drafted it would exclude plants in this region as well as Chinese ones.
- No allied country was found to have a comparable measure.
- If the rule turns on place of manufacture and not ownership, Chinese-owned plants in South-East Asia qualify, and the advantage of clean ownership largely disappears.
- The study's own judgement put a widely adopted ownership-based rule by 2028 at low likelihood. That is an opinion, not a sourced estimate.

**What this means.** The premise is a view about the direction of policy and customer behaviour over a decade. Today's evidence shows movement in that direction and no rule that delivers it. Voluntary customer preference, which may matter more than regulation, was not researched: it would need conversations with buyers.

## What was not examined

- **Private companies.** Thirteen were profiled, all Japanese, from their websites. Korean and Taiwanese registries and industry lists could not be opened.
- **Most sources.** At most about a third were opened, and many of those only in part or as tool summaries.
- **Primary evidence.** No company, customer, adviser or industry expert was contacted. No paid market research or company database was used.
- **Whole areas:** sensing, medical, industrial and defence photonics; non-AI optics as an investment case; services and distribution.
- **Shareholder look-through** for any company.

## The one lead

LandMark Optoelectronics (Taiwan), a maker of laser wafers.

- Its stated target is 2.5 times output in 2027 and again in 2028, with signed orders above this year's revenue.
- On company evidence, a central case returns about 1.6x at NT$1,900 a share, and 2.0x if bought near NT$1,550.
- It has a stated plan to place up to 4.4% of its shares with strategic investors.
- Against it: 71% of sales go to Chinese customers, which is a direct exposure if the premise holds, since those customers are the ones buyers would turn away from; its ownership and manufacturing check is provisional; US$500m buys under 10% at the current price; the case fails if wafer prices fall about 5% a year.

A second wafer maker, VPEC, was examined and does not work at current prices.

## What a conclusive test would need

1. A company database covering private firms in Japan, Korea and Taiwan, with shareholders and accounts.
2. Direct conversations with companies, their customers and sector advisers, especially on prices after 2027 and on whether buyers already favour suppliers free of Chinese ties.
3. A sourced demand and supply model by step, to 2035.
4. Agreed return target and holding period.

## For the reader to consider

- Is the opportunity strong enough to fund that fuller study?
- How strongly do we hold the premise, and over what period? The answer decides whether scarcity of clean capacity is an opportunity now or a position to build towards.
- Should the minority-only stance or the cheque size be revisited, given how much each narrowed the field?
- Should LandMark be tracked against the entry price above while that study is decided?

*Method: AI-assisted desk research from public sources, 12–16 November 2026. About a third of logged sources were opened in full or in part; the rest were seen as search results. Figures come from the study's own outputs. Share prices were moving up to 10% a day at the time of writing.*
